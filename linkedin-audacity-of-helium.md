# LinkedIn post: The Audacity of Helium

In 1908, the world's usable supply of purified helium was about 25 party balloons.

No helium industry. No supplier. Heike Kamerlingh Onnes extracted it from radioactive sand and turned it into 60 mL of liquid, roughly a teacup.

That teacup opened the path to superconductivity, MRI, and quantum computing.

But here is the part nobody talks about:

Onnes also cooled his helium below 2.17 K and observed something strange. The liquid stopped boiling. The surface went perfectly still.

He noted it and moved on.

Decades later, scientists realized he had witnessed the onset of superfluidity: a state where liquid climbs container walls, flows over the rim, and passes through molecular-scale cracks with zero viscosity.

Onnes was not lacking in brilliance. He was lacking the right experiment. He had no instrument designed to reveal what was already happening in front of him.

---

This is the exact position AI engineering is in right now.

Teams deploy agents that "work." But ask the hard questions:

- Does your agent ever exceed its permissions?
- When it loops, what stops it before the bill arrives?
- When it hallucinates confidently, what catches it?
- Does data leak across context boundaries?

The honest answer, for most teams, is: "We'd probably notice if something went really wrong."

That is Onnes watching the boiling stop and writing "peculiar behavior" in his notebook.

The next breakthroughs in AI will come less from bigger models and more from better instruments: evaluations, observability, governance, cost ceilings, rollback.

We are in the era of 25 balloons. The teams that win will not be the ones with the most helium. They will be the ones who build the laboratory.

When your agent's output contradicts your expectations, do not normalize the anomaly. Design the next experiment.
